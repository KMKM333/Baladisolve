How the five fit together

A municipal problem needs five different things to become a finished, believable project — someone to report it, and four roles no two of which can safely sit in the same hands.

  • ResidentsReport the problem on Baladi Map, or file a request here directly, and confirm each other’s reports. The confirmations are the affected group, which follows the request into its stakeholder chat and can say “I’ve been affected too”.
  • GovernmentCertifies that the request is real, lawful and within its jurisdiction — and never touches the money it approves.
  • Expert organisationsSupply the technical knowledge the problem actually needs, set their own price, and do the work.
  • SponsorsFund it, into independent escrow, before anything begins.
  • Independent verifiersAssigned when the request is certified. They sign it off with government before it can open for pledges, a second-line verifier reviews that sign-off without seeing a name, and they gate every release. Barred by construction from being the certifying body, the implementing organisation or any funder.

The first three all work directly on the project. They propose it, build it and pay for it, and every one of them has something riding on it being called a success. That is precisely why the fourth exists: it works on the process instead. The first three make the project happen; the fourth is the reason anyone outside it should believe that it did.

Read the lines, not the boxes. A Baladi Map request, confirmed by residents, goes to government and nowhere else until it is certified. Three roles then connect to the request — they certify it, build it and pay for it. The first-line verifier touches it only as a gate, three times: it signs off the request before a single pledge is taken, it gates every release, and it closes the ledger. Above it sits the second line, which checks the first: every sign-off, one release in ten, every closure — seeing no names. Neither certifies, builds or pays, and that is why they can be believed about work they had no part in.

Nothing here is released on a claim. A milestone stays “submitted” until someone with no stake in it confirms it happened.

Who does the verifying

The fourth role — and the reason the other three can be believed.

A verifier confirms that a milestone actually happened before any money moves, and is structurally barred from being the body that certified the request, the organisation doing the work, or anyone funding it. Independence is not a box someone ticks: five tests run against each request’s own records, and the results are shown on the request itself with their outcomes.

Where they fit — and why the timing is the whole point

A verifier is assigned at certification: before a single pledge, and long before any work. They sign off the request, a second-line verifier reviews that sign-off without seeing a name, and every release waits on the first verifier again.

1 Request certified, verifier assigned

Rotated from the pool that passes the independence tests for this request, and recorded publicly with who assigned them.

2 Request signed off

The verifier confirms the milestones and the evidence each will need, and sets the cost bracket. Government certifies scope and jurisdiction, never price.

3 Second-line review

A senior verifier reviews that sign-off seeing no names, and confirms it or sends it back. Only then does the request open for pledges.

4 Escrow confirmed, work starts

The implementer already knows who will check them — and could not choose that person. Every tranche waits on their sign-off.

Bring the verifier in after the money is committed and funders are backing a request nobody has confirmed can be checked. Bring them in after the work is done and the party being checked is picking their own examiner. That ordering is the difference between verification and a formality, which is why it sits in the workflow as its own step rather than as an implementation detail.

Optional: a contract layer on top

Where a sponsor or a municipality wants a stronger guarantee than a promise, the release rules can be written into a smart contract: funds move only on signatures from assigned verifiers, the signer list excludes the implementer, certifier and funders by construction, the tranche schedule is fixed when funding closes, and unreleased money returns automatically if a request stalls.

What it does not do: a contract cannot know whether the wall was built. Someone still has to look. It makes the process tamper-evident — who signed, when, and that nobody moved money alone — not the underlying facts true. It is an added layer for those who want it, never a replacement for the verifier.

When a request doesn’t finish

The stages nobody puts on a homepage — and where the money sits at each one.

Escrow is not a holding pen. It exists so that money which has not bought anything can go back. A request can end without finishing at four different points, and each one has a defined answer for the funds — because “what happens if it fails” is the first question a serious donor asks, and the first question a regulator asks.

Open question — partly closed

But who checks the verifier?

Everything above rests on one role. The verifier is the reason a certification can be believed, a sponsor’s money is safe, and a finished wall is really finished. So it is fair to ask the obvious next question: if the verifier checks everyone else, who checks them?

Now: a second-line verifier. Every first-line sign-off — the decision that lets a request open for pledges — is reviewed by a second, more senior verifier who sees the decision and the reasoning but no names. Every later change to cost or timeline goes through the same pair. Their record sits on the register under a pseudonym. Milestone releases are checked once by the first line, and one in ten is re-checked afterwards by the second line, which also reviews every closing report. Consequences are written too: fall below a published line and you drop a tier, and the way back is a training course run with project stakeholders. The course exists for each line, and every role has a handbook.

This is not a hypothetical worry. The closest thing to Manāra that already exists at scale is the carbon credit market, where independent auditors sign off before money moves. It went wrong, and the numbers are public.

2 in 3Accredited auditors who missed the flaws in projects later found to be overstated, across 95 projects studied.
84%Of credits across 2,346 projects that did not represent real cuts — all of them signed off by someone independent.
4Verification bodies suspended in 2025 — caught by a whistleblower, not by routine monitoring.

The cause everyone landed on was simple: the auditors were paid by the people they were checking, and the people being checked got to pick them. Manāra was built to avoid exactly that — two of the three doors are already shut.

What an account page should be

Where each role lands after registering. Drawn from three families of product that already solve part of this — crowdfunding portals, CRMs, and social profiles — each of which answers a different question.

Starting point

Manāra already has seven of these

Reachable today from Join → “Preview the dashboard”, or . All seven share one shape: a title, four stat tiles, a table of recent rows, and a list of standing obligations. What is missing is the connection — registering does not land you on one.

Research

Three families, three different questions

These are not three styles of the same page. Each answers something different, and the four roles need different mixes.

62%Of affluent donors evaluate the impact of their giving before deciding on the next gift. A sponsor’s page is a retention mechanism, not a receipt.
80%Of day-one persuasion is done by the empty state. Every new account here has zero requests, pledges and verifications.

Option A

One shell, four lenses

Keep the shape the seven demos already use — stats, table, obligations — and swap only the contents per role.

Gain. Cheapest by a distance, already half built, and consistent across every role.
Cost. Forces a sponsor’s impact story into a work-queue layout, which is the wrong shape for the one role that is not doing any work.
Option B — recommended

Private queue, public twin

Two zones: what needs you (private, CRM-style) sitting on top of your public record — the page everyone else already sees of you. An account becomes an authenticated overlay on a public identity rather than a separate private space.

Gain. The pattern already exists here: the verifier register is a public profile, and organisation and municipality pages already render publicly. This generalises them rather than inventing something. It is also the only option that is on-brand — a platform whose whole argument is public track record should not have a purely private account page.
Cost. Two zones to design per role instead of one, and it commits to every account showing its own bad numbers.
Option C

Ledger first

The account page is a filtered view of the public ledger — your slice of exactly the data anyone else can read.

Gain. Maximally consistent with the transparency claim, and it would force the browsable ledger to finally get built — the site says “open ledger” repeatedly and has no page behind it.
Cost. Badly under-serves “what needs me today”, which is the thing that makes anyone come back a second time.
Option D

Progressive

Day one is an onboarding checklist that turns into a dashboard as it fills.

Gain. Directly answers the empty-state problem, which is otherwise the day-one experience for every single new account.
Cost. Not really an alternative to A, B or C — it is a layer any of them needs anyway.

What to build: B, with D layered on it

One page per role, three bands. The same skeleton everywhere, so a reader who has seen one account page can read any of them.

1
Needs you nowThe queue — milestones awaiting a decision, requests awaiting certification, tranches awaiting release. Empty on day one, so it carries the onboarding checklist instead: get vetted, then post your first request. That is where option D lives.
2
Your recordThe public twin, credibility signals first. A verifier sees approvals, rejections and overturns; a partner sees completion rate and days-to-milestone; a sponsor sees funded and verified totals. The same numbers a stranger sees, because they are the same page.
3
Where the money isOnly for the roles that touch it. Sponsors get the impact framing — what changed, who benefited, how do you know. Municipalities and experts get escrow and tranche state, held against released.

One deliberate consequence: every account page shows its own bad numbers — rejections, overturns, stalled requests. That follows from what the site already does in publishing rejection counts next to approvals, and it is what the impact-reporting research says actually builds trust.

Sources: Zeffy on nonprofit transparency · Casebook on impact reports · HubSpot dashboard patterns · Empty states as onboarding · Profile page design

Verifier register

Every accredited verifier on the platform, their record side by side. Sort any column — the numbers are the same ones shown on each request, gathered in one place so they can be compared.

Second-line verifiers

Appointed from the table above, not recruited: a current licence, at least thirty decisions, and an overturn rate in the bottom half. They review the first-line verifier’s sign-off on every request before it opens for pledges, and every change to cost or timeline after — pseudonymously, seeing no names. The record is published under the pseudonym.

Variances approved

Every change to a published cost or timeline, on one page, with the request, the change and both verifiers who approved it. It is the cheapest fraud signal a ledger can carry: a verifier who approves many variances for the same implementer stands out here before anyone complains.

Rejection rateShare of milestones sent back rather than passed. Neither a high nor a low number is automatically good — but a number far from everyone else’s is worth asking about.
OverturnedDecisions later found to be wrong and reversed. A zero is only meaningful if something was actually looking.
Median daysEvidence submitted to decision made. Fast is not free: somebody has to go and look.
LicenceAccreditation number and the date it lapses. Past that date a verifier cannot be assigned.
VariancesChanges to a published cost or timeline this verifier approved. Many for one implementer is the thing to ask about.
Agreement rateShare of first-line sign-offs a second-line verifier confirmed rather than sent back. Same caveat as rejection rate: only a number far from the others means anything.

What this page does not do. It publishes the record. The second-line table is where sign-offs and variances get a second opinion. Releases get one check, and one in ten gets a second afterwards. An outlier in the first table now carries a consequence in the standing column, under the rules above.
