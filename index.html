The network

Four roles, each with a defined job. Certification, expertise, funding and verification never sit in the same hands — the fourth exists precisely so the first three can be checked by someone with no stake in them.

How they operate together

A municipal problem needs four different things to become a finished, believable project, and no two of them can safely sit in the same hands. Government certifies it, experts solve it, sponsors fund it — and all three of those work directly on the project. They propose it, build it and pay for it, and every one of them has something riding on it being called a success.

That is precisely why the fourth exists. An independent verifier works on the process rather than the project: assigned before work begins, barred by construction from being the certifying body, the implementing organisation or any funder of that request, and the only party who can release a tranche of escrow. The first three make the project happen; the fourth is the reason anyone outside it should believe that it did.

Read the lines, not the boxes. Three roles connect to the request — they certify it, build it and pay for it. The fourth has no line to the request at all: it sits on the release, and nothing passes that point without it. That gap is the separation, and it is why a verifier can be believed about work they had no part in.

Nothing here is released on a claim. A milestone stays “submitted” until someone with no stake in it confirms it happened.

Who steps in, and when

Every request below passes through the same four hands in the same order — and the order is the safeguard, not a formality.

When each role is brought in — and why the timing matters

Three roles work directly on the project. The fourth works on the process, and is brought in before the first of them can start.

1 Government Certified before the request is public

Checked for jurisdiction, code and necessity before a request can open for pledges — so nobody is asked to fund what no authority has confirmed.

2 Experts Matched before a budget is fixed

The field a problem needs is settled before anyone prices it, so scope is argued on technical grounds rather than on who happened to bid.

3 Sponsors Captured into escrow before work starts

Pledges are secured with an independent custodian before day one, so no implementer starts on a promise. Held, not spent.

4 Verifier Assigned before any work begins

The moment escrow is confirmed, a reviewer with no tie to the certifier, implementer or funders is assigned. Only then does work start.

Change the order and each safeguard quietly stops working. Certify after the pledges and funders are backing an unapproved request. Fix the budget before the field is known and the price is a negotiation rather than an estimate. Assign the verifier after the work is done and the party being checked is picking their own examiner. Each of these steps only does its job because of where it sits in the sequence — which is why the sequence is published rather than assumed.

Who does the verifying

The fourth role — and the reason the other three can be believed.

A verifier confirms that a milestone actually happened before any money moves, and is structurally barred from being the body that certified the request, the organisation doing the work, or anyone funding it. Independence is not a box someone ticks: five tests run against each request’s own records, and the results are shown on the request itself with their outcomes.

Where they fit — and why the timing is the whole point

A verifier is assigned at escrow confirmation: after the money is secured, but before any work begins.

1
Escrow confirmedFunds are held. Nothing is released.
2
Verifier assignedRotated from the pool that passes the independence tests for this request. Recorded publicly, with who assigned them.
3
Work startsThe implementer already knows who will check them, and could not choose that person.

Assign the verifier after the work is done and the party being checked is picking their own examiner. That single ordering decision is the difference between verification and a formality — which is why it sits in the workflow as its own step rather than as an implementation detail.

Optional: a contract layer on top

Where a sponsor or a municipality wants a stronger guarantee than a promise, the release rules can be written into a smart contract: funds move only on signatures from assigned verifiers, the signer list excludes the implementer, certifier and funders by construction, the tranche schedule is fixed when funding closes, and unreleased money returns automatically if a request stalls.

What it does not do: a contract cannot know whether the wall was built. Someone still has to look. It makes the process tamper-evident — who signed, when, and that nobody moved money alone — not the underlying facts true. It is an added layer for those who want it, never a replacement for the verifier.

Open question — not solved yet

But who checks the verifier?

Everything above rests on one role. The verifier is the reason a certification can be believed, a sponsor’s money is safe, and a finished wall is really finished. So it is fair to ask the obvious next question: if the verifier checks everyone else, who checks them?

Right now, honestly: nobody. A verifier’s rejections are published next to their approvals, and that is the whole safeguard. It is better than nothing — a reviewer who has never rejected anything is visible — but it is not a system. We would rather write that down here than let you assume it was handled.

This is not a hypothetical worry. The closest thing to Manāra that already exists at scale is the carbon credit market, where independent auditors sign off before money moves. It went wrong, and the numbers are public.

2 in 3Accredited auditors who missed the flaws in projects later found to be overstated, across 95 projects studied.
84%Of credits across 2,346 projects that did not represent real cuts — all of them signed off by someone independent.
4Verification bodies suspended in 2025 — caught by a whistleblower, not by routine monitoring.

The cause everyone landed on was simple: the auditors were paid by the people they were checking, and the people being checked got to pick them. Manāra was built to avoid exactly that — two of the three doors are already shut.

How the money is actually verified

Six steps, each one checkable.

01 · INTAKE

A named, checkable need

Requests come from a registered municipality, a vetted NGO, or a recognised business partner — many grounded in a citizen report already confirmed by other affected residents.

02 · VETTING

Whole-partner review

Before a request goes live, the implementing partner is checked once — legal registration, past project record, references.

03 · ESCROW

Money moves, but doesn't land yet

Pledges sit in escrow held by an independent custodian — never a government account, never the implementer's — until a milestone is verified.

04 · VERIFICATION

A claim and a check are different steps

Evidence sits as "submitted" until an unaffiliated verifier confirms it. Only then does the ledger mark it verified and escrow release.

05 · OPEN LEDGER

Anyone can trace any lira

Funders — named or anonymous — see the same trail everyone else does. No private reporting track.

06 · DISPUTE

A report button that goes somewhere

Flags route to an independent oversight partner, not to the implementer being flagged.

Design constraint worth stating out loud: this platform should never claim that one specific dollar funded one specific brick. Pooled milestone funding is more honest, and more auditable, than manufacturing a person-to-person link that isn't really there.

When a request doesn’t finish

The stages nobody puts on a homepage — and where the money sits at each one.

Escrow is not a holding pen. It exists so that money which has not bought anything can go back. A request can end without finishing at four different points, and each one has a defined answer for the funds — because “what happens if it fails” is the first question a serious donor asks, and the first question a regulator asks.

StageWhere the money isCan exit to
SubmittedNothing pledgedCertified, or refused
Open for pledgesPledged, not capturedThreshold met, or lapsed → refunded in full
Escrow fundedHeld, not releasableVerifier assigned, work starts
In progressHeld, released per verified milestoneNext milestone, or stalled → balance returned
Milestone rejectedHeld — nothing releasedRemediation, then resubmission
ClosedFully releasedFinal report published

A rejected milestone is not a dead request. It returns to the implementer for remediation and resubmission — because if rejection meant the project died, no verifier would ever reject anything, and the check would quietly become a formality.
